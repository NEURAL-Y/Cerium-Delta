The kidney of Rana temporaria (European common frog)
What the kidney looks like and where it sits
The frog has two kidneys. They sit flat against the back wall of the body cavity, one on each side of the spine, just below the heart and stomach. They are dark reddish-brown, elongated, and somewhat bean-shaped. If you open a frog's belly, the kidneys are the two dark strips you see pressed against the dorsal body wall. In males, you will notice yellow-orange streaks running along the front surface of each kidney — those are the adrenal glands, called interrenal glands in frogs, which produce hormones like aldosterone and corticosterone.
The kidneys drain urine through two tubes called ureters, one from each kidney, which run down and empty into the cloaca — the single shared chamber at the rear of the frog that handles waste from the gut, urine from the kidneys, and eggs or sperm from the reproductive organs. In male frogs, the ureter also carries sperm, so it serves a dual reproductive and excretory function. This is a primitive vertebrate arrangement that mammals have long since separated into distinct tubes.

The type of kidney a frog has
Your own kidneys are metanephric kidneys — the most evolutionarily advanced type, found only in reptiles, birds, and mammals. A frog's kidney is an opisthonephros, an older and more primitive design that sits phylogenetically between the embryonic pronephros (which only tadpoles briefly use right at hatching) and the mammalian metanephros. The opisthonephros develops from a longer stretch of the embryonic tissue called the nephric ridge, and it retains some features that your kidneys shed long ago in vertebrate evolution — most notably direct connections to the body cavity and a dual blood supply.

The nephron — the filtering unit
Every kidney is made of thousands of tiny tubes called nephrons. Each nephron is the actual machine that filters the blood, reclaims what the body needs, and sends the rest out as urine. In Rana temporaria, each nephron has the following parts working in sequence:
The renal corpuscle. This is the filtration capsule at the start of every nephron. It contains a glomerulus, which is a tight ball of tiny blood vessels with porous walls. Blood pressure forces water, salts, glucose, urea, and other small molecules out of the blood and into the space inside a surrounding cup called Bowman's capsule. Large molecules like proteins and blood cells are too big to pass through and stay in the blood. The inner wall of Bowman's capsule is made of special cells called podocytes, which have elaborate interlocking foot-like extensions that create precise-sized filtration slits — a second layer of selectivity after the porous capillary wall.
Rana temporaria has unusually large glomeruli for its body size. This is not an accident. The frog's skin is permeable to water, meaning when the frog sits in a pond, water floods into its body continuously by osmosis. The kidneys must produce enormous volumes of dilute urine to expel all this incoming water. A large glomerulus with a high filtration rate makes this possible.
The nephrostome. Here is where the frog kidney becomes genuinely strange compared to anything in a mammal. Each nephron has a nephrostome — a small, ciliated, funnel-shaped opening that connects directly to the coelom, the fluid-filled body cavity surrounding the organs. Tiny beating cilia draw coelomic fluid directly into the nephron. This is a relic of the most ancient vertebrate kidney design, in which filtration happened from the body cavity fluid rather than directly from blood. In modern Rana temporaria, the nephrostome is mostly a structural leftover — most filtration happens at the glomerulus — but when blood pressure drops severely, the nephrostome may become a fallback filtration route.
The proximal convoluted tubule. After filtration, the fluid (called filtrate) flows into the first and longest tubular section, the proximal convoluted tubule or PCT. The cells lining this tube are packed with mitochondria and covered on their inner surface with thousands of tiny finger-like projections called microvilli, which together form what is called a brush border. This brush border multiplies the surface area of the tube by up to forty times, giving the cells a massive working surface to pull molecules back out of the filtrate and return them to the blood.
In the PCT, the frog kidney reclaims virtually all of the glucose that was filtered (using protein transporters that co-transport glucose alongside sodium ions), all amino acids, roughly sixty-five percent of the sodium ions filtered, and water that follows the sodium by osmosis. Bicarbonate is reclaimed here too, keeping the blood's pH stable. The PCT is the most metabolically expensive part of the kidney — all that sodium pumping requires enormous amounts of ATP from the mitochondria.
The intermediate segment. After the PCT, the nephron enters a short, thinner section called the intermediate segment. In mammals, this region is massively extended and folded into a hairpin shape called the loop of Henle, which descends deep into a region of the kidney called the medulla and creates a powerful osmotic gradient. This gradient is what allows mammals to produce concentrated urine — far more concentrated than the blood — which is essential for surviving on land without constant access to water.
Rana temporaria does not have a loop of Henle. The intermediate segment is short and unelaborated. This is the single most important limitation of the frog kidney. Without the loop of Henle and the medullary gradient it creates, the frog kidney physically cannot concentrate urine above approximately 300 milliosmoles per kilogram — roughly the same concentration as the blood itself. A human kidney can concentrate urine to about 1,200 milliosmoles per kilogram. Some desert mammals can reach 9,000. The frog simply cannot do this. This is why frogs cannot survive in dry environments the way mammals can — they lose water through their skin faster than the kidney can compensate, because the kidney can only produce urine as dilute as or slightly more dilute than blood, never more concentrated.
The distal convoluted tubule. After the intermediate segment, the nephron widens into the distal convoluted tubule or DCT. This is where hormonal fine-tuning of ion balance happens. The hormone aldosterone, produced by the interrenal gland sitting right on top of the kidney, travels through the blood to the DCT and instructs the cells to insert more sodium channels and sodium pumps into their membranes. This causes more sodium to be reclaimed from the urine back into the blood — tightening the sodium balance especially when the frog is on land and cannot afford to waste ions in urine. As sodium is pulled back in, potassium is secreted out into the urine to maintain electrical balance. The DCT also participates in acid-base regulation by secreting hydrogen ions or ammonium into the urine.
The collecting duct. Multiple nephrons feed their output into a larger shared tube called the collecting duct. This is where one of the most important hormones in frog physiology acts. When a frog is dehydrated — sitting on dry land, losing water through its skin faster than it can absorb it — the brain signals the posterior pituitary gland to release a hormone called arginine vasotocin, or AVT. AVT is the amphibian equivalent of the antidiuretic hormone (ADH) found in mammals, and the two hormones are evolutionarily directly related.
AVT travels through the blood to the collecting duct cells and triggers a cascade inside those cells that causes water-channel proteins called aquaporins to be inserted into the wall of the duct facing the urine. These aquaporin channels allow water to flow rapidly out of the urine and back into the surrounding tissue and then into the blood. The result is that urine output drops sharply and the frog rehydrates itself from within. This AVT-aquaporin system is ancient — it exists in fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals, with the same basic molecular logic operating across 400 million years of evolution.

The urinary bladder — a frog's secret water tank
Rana temporaria has a large, thin-walled urinary bladder connected to the cloaca. In mammals, the bladder is purely a storage bag for urine before elimination. In frogs, it serves an additional function that is quite extraordinary: it is a water reservoir.
When a frog is well hydrated, dilute urine accumulates in the bladder. When the frog moves onto dry land and begins losing water through its permeable skin, AVT triggers aquaporin insertion not only in the collecting duct but also in the bladder wall. The frog then absorbs water back from its own stored urine through the bladder wall and into the bloodstream. It is essentially recycling its own urine for water.
This mechanism is so effective that some burrowing frog species can remain underground in a dormant state called estivation for months or years, surviving purely on water reabsorbed from their bladder. Rana temporaria uses a milder version of this, allowing it to venture further from water than its skin permeability alone would permit.

The dual blood supply
The frog kidney receives blood from two completely separate sources, which is fundamentally different from mammalian kidneys. Mammals have only one blood supply to the kidney — the renal artery, bringing oxygenated blood to the glomeruli for filtration.
Rana temporaria has the renal artery system, yes, but it also has a renal portal system. Venous blood returning from the hindlimbs travels via a renal portal vein directly into capillaries that wrap around the tubules of the nephron, before continuing on to the heart. This portal blood never passes through the glomeruli. It does not get filtered. Instead, it supplies the tubule cells with oxygen and nutrients, and the tubule cells can actively secrete certain waste substances — drugs, toxins, organic acids — directly from this portal blood into the tubular fluid, adding them to the urine without needing them to pass through the glomerular filtration step first.
This dual supply means the frog kidney is simultaneously doing filtration from renal artery blood at the glomerulus and secretion from portal blood at the tubules, using two independent blood sources. Mammals lost the renal portal system entirely during evolution.

Osmoregulation — how the kidney shifts modes
The kidney of Rana temporaria does not work the same way all the time. It operates in two distinct modes depending on whether the frog is in water or on land.
When the frog is sitting in a pond, its body fluids at roughly 240 milliosmoles per kilogram are far more concentrated than the fresh water around it at around 5 to 30 milliosmoles per kilogram. Water rushes in continuously through the skin by osmosis. The kidney responds by opening the afferent arterioles wide, flooding the glomeruli with blood and producing an enormous volume of very dilute urine — as low as 30 to 50 milliosmoles per kilogram. The tubules actively pump sodium back into the blood as fast as possible. Simultaneously, special cells in the skin scavenge sodium ions directly from the pond water and pump them into the blood, partially replacing the sodium being lost in urine.
When the frog moves onto land, the situation reverses. Now the threat is desiccation, not dilution. The afferent arterioles constrict, reducing blood flow to the glomeruli and sharply cutting the filtration rate. AVT rises, inserting aquaporins in the collecting duct and bladder. Urine output drops to nearly nothing. The frog draws water back from its bladder reserve. Some terrestrial frogs also shift their nitrogen excretion from ammonia (which requires a lot of water to dilute and excrete) to urea (which is less toxic and can be excreted in smaller volumes of water).

Nitrogenous waste
Frog tadpoles live entirely underwater and excrete their nitrogen waste as ammonia. Ammonia is highly toxic but very water-soluble and cheap to produce, so it simply diffuses out across the gills into the surrounding water. Adult Rana temporaria excretes a mixture of ammonia and urea depending on hydration status and how much time is spent on land versus in water. Fully terrestrial frogs shift mostly to urea because it is far less toxic per unit of nitrogen and requires less water to carry out of the body. The kidney then filters urea from the blood and excretes it in urine, though some urea is reabsorbed in the tubules — particularly during estivation in burrowing species, when urea accumulates in the body fluids at very high concentrations and is only released when the rains return.

Hormonal control summary
Arginine vasotocin from the posterior pituitary drives water reabsorption in the collecting duct and bladder when the frog is dehydrated. Aldosterone from the interrenal gland on top of the kidney drives sodium reabsorption in the distal tubule, particularly when sodium levels in the blood fall. Prolactin from the anterior pituitary enhances sodium retention and is especially active when the frog returns to water for breeding — it helps the frog shift back into the high-filtration, sodium-conserving freshwater mode. Atrial natriuretic peptide released from heart muscle when blood volume is too high does the opposite — it increases glomerular filtration rate and reduces tubular sodium reabsorption, causing the frog to dump more sodium and water into the urine and reduce blood volume.

Why this matters beyond the frog
Every molecular system described above is evolutionarily continuous with what operates in your own kidneys. The aquaporin channels that AVT inserts into the collecting duct are the same family of proteins that ADH inserts into your collecting duct. The sodium-glucose cotransporters in the frog PCT are structurally nearly identical to the ones in your PCT — and they are the exact proteins targeted by SGLT2 inhibitor drugs used to treat diabetes in humans. The aldosterone-sodium channel axis in the DCT is the same system that blood pressure medications called mineralocorticoid antagonists target. The Rana temporaria kidney, sitting in a frog you can find in any European garden pond, is running a version of the same ancient program your kidneys run — just without the loop of Henle, and with the extra twist of a water-storing bladder and a portal blood supply that vertebrate evolution eventually decided to leave behind.
